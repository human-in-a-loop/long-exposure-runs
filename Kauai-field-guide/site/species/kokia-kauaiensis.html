Kokia kauaiensis (Rock) O.Deg. & Duvel

kokiʻo, kokiʻo ʻula · Kauai treecotton, Kauai kokiʻo

Quick facts

FamilyMalvaceae
Scientific nameKokia kauaiensis (Rock) O.Deg. & Duvel
Hawaiian name(s)kokiʻo, kokiʻo ʻula
English name(s)Kauai treecotton, Kauai kokiʻo
StatusEndemic
ConservationUS Endangered (USFWS 1996; 5-Year Review 2017 documents severe decline — from ca. 145 trees at listing to ca. 19 wild individuals reported by USFWS); IUCN Critically Endangered (assessment updated 2020, Heintzman, Nyberg & Wood)
Coastal zonesValley mouth Sea cliff
OccurrenceEndemic to west and northwest Kauaʻi. Documented populations occur in Paʻaiki, Mahanaloa, Kuia, and Kalalau Valleys, in Nā Pali Coast State Park, in Pōhākuao, and in the Koaiʻe Stream branch of Waimea Canyon — all on State-owned land. Sits at the boundary of the guide's coastal scope: most individuals grow at 350–660 m in mesic forest with lama (*Diospyros sandwicensis*) and ʻōhiʻa (*Metrosideros polymorpha*), reaching down toward the upper reaches of Nā Pali valleys visible from Kalalau Trail vantage points. A hiker will almost never encounter a wild plant; the species is included as an emblematic Nā Pali cliff-and-valley endemic and a visual anchor for Kauaʻi's Malvaceae diversity.
Uncertainty: Population and individual-count numbers vary between publications (typically 100–200 wild plants across 6–11 populations depending on survey year). Treat any specific count as approximate; refer to current NTBG and USFWS documentation for authoritative figures. Do not approach or handle any wild individual — federally listed, and the terrain is dangerous.

How to identify

Growth form
Small deciduous tree 5–10 m tall with a broad open crown; drops its leaves seasonally, which is unusual among Hawaiian native trees and helps distinguish the genus in the field.
Size
5–10 m tall; trunk usually 15–30 cm diameter.
Leaves
Large, palmately lobed, roughly circular in outline, 12–25 cm wide, with seven or nine deep lobes and a heart-shaped (cordate) base; petiole long, blade texture leathery. Deciduous, so mature trees may be leafless in dry periods.
Flowers
Spectacular — the diagnostic feature. Five thick red petals arranged in a distinct spiral / twist, ~7–10 cm across, with a prominent central column (the fused staminal tube typical of Malvaceae) that projects and curves. Unlike garden hibiscus, the petals stay overlapped and twisted, giving the flower a pinwheel silhouette.
Fruit / seed
A dry capsule, 3–5 cm long, opening at maturity into five valves and releasing dark seeds surrounded by short reddish fibres.
Bark / stem
Grey-brown, becoming shallowly furrowed on older trunks; slender for the crown size.
Where it sits on the coast
Upper reaches of Nā Pali valleys and adjacent mesic forest ridges at 350–660 m. Not on beaches, not in the strand. If you are on the coast proper, you are almost certainly not looking at wild Kokia kauaiensis; if you are hiking Kalalau or looking across a Nā Pali valley, an isolated deciduous tree with big palmately-lobed leaves and a spiral red flower would be a candidate.

Clinchers (features that lock the ID)

  • Big palmately 7–9-lobed roughly circular leaves with cordate base — no other Nā Pali native tree looks like this.
  • Spectacular spiral / twisted red flower with a projecting curved central column (diagnostic; visible only in flower).
  • Small deciduous tree in Kauaʻi west/NW mesic forest; other native trees at this elevation are largely evergreen.
  • Endemic to Kauaʻi — off-island geography alone rules out this species.

Look-alikes

  • Hibiscus waimeae subsp. hannerae (in this guide) — White flowers fading pink (not red) with a slender pink staminal column; leaves ovate, not palmately lobed; small evergreen tree of moist Nā Pali valley bottoms rather than upper mesic ridges.
  • Hibiscus rockii and other native / cultivated red hibiscuses — Hibiscus petals open flat (not spiraled) and do not stay twisted; leaves are simple ovate to lobed but not palmately 7–9-lobed on a circular blade with a cordate base. If the flower opens flat it is not Kokia.
  • Kokia drynarioides (Hawaiʻi Island kokiʻo) and Kokia cookei (Molokaʻi) — Sister species on other islands, similar spiral red flower. Off-island geography settles it — wild Kokia kauaiensis is the only *Kokia* on Kauaʻi.

Ecology

Long-lived Kauaʻi mesic-forest endemic. Population declines have been driven by feral ungulates, landslides in the Nā Pali drainages, invasive plants, and fire. Kokia species have unusually low genetic diversity for the number of individuals extant, so seed banking and ex situ cultivation (NTBG, other botanical gardens) are central to recovery. Holocene fossil seed capsules suggest the species had a much wider historical range before human-era pressures. [1], [2], [3], [5], [53], [57], [58]

Cultural significance

Kokia species were used historically as sources of red / brown dye from the bark; this guide records the association without dye- extraction instructions, which properly belong to Hawaiian practitioners. Cultural documentation specific to *K. kauaiensis* (as opposed to the more widely-cited *K. drynarioides*) is sparse in the accessible literature — we note the association honestly rather than inventing use claims.
