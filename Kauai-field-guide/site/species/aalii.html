Dodonaea viscosa Jacq.

ʻaʻaliʻi · hopbush, hopseed bush

Quick facts

FamilySapindaceae
Scientific nameDodonaea viscosa Jacq.
Hawaiian name(s)ʻaʻaliʻi
English name(s)hopbush, hopseed bush
StatusIndigenous (native, non-endemic)
Conservation—
Coastal zonesSea cliff Valley mouth
OccurrenceWidespread on the dry Nā Pali–Kona coastal cliffs and valley mouths; a common woody component of dry lowland shrubland from the coast up into the ridges. Tolerates wind, salt spray, and thin cliff-face soils.

How to identify

Growth form
Small shrub to small tree, 1–8 m; usually seen on the coast as a dense multi-stemmed shrub 1–3 m high.
Size
1–3 m as a coastal shrub; up to 8 m in sheltered sites.
Leaves
Alternate, simple, narrow lanceolate to oblanceolate, 4–10 cm long, tapering at both ends. Surface often sticky-resinous to the touch when young (the species epithet *viscosa* means "sticky"); frequently yellow-green rather than deep green.
Flowers
Small greenish or yellow-green, inconspicuous, in short axillary or terminal clusters — usually overlooked next to the showy fruits.
Fruit / seed
Papery three-winged (occasionally four-winged) capsule ~1.5 cm across, ranging from bright red to pink to pale yellow depending on plant and ripening stage; often clustered densely along shoot tips and persisting for weeks. Contains a small black seed in the middle chamber. **The fruit — not the flower — is what carries the plant's colour**, and at a distance a fruiting ʻaʻaliʻi can be mistaken for something in heavy flower.
Bark / stem
Slender, tan-grey, with fine longitudinal ridging on older stems.
Where it sits on the coast
Sea cliffs (including exposed Nā Pali cliff faces), dry ridge shoulders, valley mouths — anywhere the soil is thin and drainage is fast.

Clinchers (features that lock the ID)

  • Papery three-winged fruit capsule in red/pink/yellow, clustered at shoot tips — unmistakable.
  • Narrow sticky-resinous leaves; young growth genuinely tacky to a fingertip.
  • Dry, wind-exposed, well-drained coastal position.
  • Colour on the plant is in the fruit, not the flower.

Look-alikes

  • Introduced legumes with reddish pods (Sesbania, Cassia) — Introduced legume pods are elongated cylinders or flattened straps that hang from the branch, not radial three-winged papery capsules clustered at shoot tips. Legume leaves are pinnately compound; ʻaʻaliʻi leaves are simple.
  • Introduced *Dodonaea* cultivars ("Purple Hopbush") — Cultivated purple-leaved forms exist in landscape use elsewhere in Hawaiʻi; wild coastal ʻaʻaliʻi on the Nā Pali cliffs is always green-leaved. If a plant with the winged fruit has consistently purple foliage in a garden setting, it is not a Nā Pali wild population.

Ecology

Pantropical and subtropical species with well-established Hawaiian populations; extremely wind- and drought-tolerant, an early colonist of exposed thin-soil sites, and a persistent component of dry lowland Hawaiian shrubland. Wagner, Herbst & Sohmer (1999) treats Hawaiian populations under *Dodonaea viscosa* sensu lato; several named forms have been described but are not consistently maintained in current treatments. Cultural significance

The bright, showy winged fruits are a distinctive natural material for lei — the fruit clusters hold their colour well and make striking strand lei. Hawaiian tradition also used the hard wood for tool handles, hoe (digging stick) shafts, and parts of weapons; historical material-culture accounts (Rock 1913, Krauss 1993) record ʻaʻaliʻi wood alongside other hard-wooded natives. This guide names the practices; the transmission of lei-making and wood-working traditions belongs to Hawaiian cultural practitioners.
